# 24. Paper That Leaves the Building

There is a shelf in Riverton's office that holds four binders, and they are the last paper the
company keeps.

They are not invoices. The invoices are in the system and in the customers' inboxes. The binders
hold the things that have to be signed: the delivery notes that came back with a caretaker's
initials, the credit notes that a customer queried, and the two contracts that matter. Everything
else is a PDF.

This chapter is about the moment a document leaves the building, which is the moment where software
meets the physical world—and where a surprising number of otherwise good systems fail.

---

## What makes a document printable

A printable document is not a screen that has been shrunk. It is a different object, with different
rules, and Odoo's report engine is worth understanding because it enforces them.

**It has a fixed layout.** A screen reflows; a page does not. The invoice template decides where the
logo goes, how wide the description column is, and what happens when a product name is longer than
the column. (The answer, in a good template, is that it wraps—and the row grows. In a bad one, it
overlaps the price, and the customer sees a number that belongs to a different line.)

**It has a header and a footer that repeat.** Page one of a five-page invoice must say whose invoice
it is, and page five must say that it is page five of five. This is not vanity: a page that arrives
alone, without a header, is a page that can be disputed.

**It carries the company's identity.** Name, address, tax number, registration, bank details. These
are not decoration; they are the fields that make the document a legal claim, and they come from the
company record configured in chapter nine.

**It is reproducible.** The same invoice, printed a year later, must look the same. That is why the
template is a template and not a document that somebody edited: an invoice that can be edited after
the fact is an invoice that proves nothing.

## The three documents a wholesaler actually prints

Riverton prints three things, and each has a different job.

**The invoice**, as a PDF attached to an email. It is the request for payment and the tax document,
and it is the one the customer's accounts department will file.

**The delivery note**, printed on the van's printer and signed at the door. It is not a financial
document; it is the evidence that goods arrived, and it is the thing that resolves the argument three
weeks later about whether the pallet was signed for.

**The statement**, sent monthly to customers with several open invoices. It is a list, not a demand,
and it is the single most effective collection tool in existence—because it puts every open item in
front of the person who pays, in one page, without an accusation.

There is a fourth, less obvious one: the **stock report**. It is not sent to anybody. It is printed
because Tom reads it in the van, and reading a thing on paper is different from reading it on a
screen—slower, and therefore more likely to be thought about.

## The workflow that replaced the envelope

Doreen's old workflow was: print, fold, address, stamp, post, file the copy. Five steps, and the
last one was the one that failed, because filing is the step that has no deadline.

The new workflow is: generate, check, send, and let the system file it. Three steps, and the filing
is automatic—which means it happens, which means that when a customer telephones in November about
an invoice from June, the answer is a search rather than an expedition to the invoice room.

The check is the step that matters, and it is the one companies skip. A document generated by a
machine is not automatically correct: it is correct if the data behind it is correct, and the data
behind it was entered by somebody. Doreen's habit, which she kept from the paper era, is to read the
first invoice of every run—not all of them, the first one—and to look at the customer's name, the
address, the total and the tax. If those four are right, the rest almost certainly are.

## What the assistant does, and what it does not

The assistant can produce the PDF and attach it to an email. At Riverton it does not, and the
decision is worth recording because it is a choice, not a limitation.

The assistant's role is to *prepare*: create the invoice, post it, produce the document, and tell
Doreen that it is ready. The sending is done by a person, in the mail client, because sending is the
step where a mistake reaches a customer—and a mistake that reaches a customer costs more than the
minute it takes to prevent it.

That is a conservative choice, and a company with a hundred invoices a day cannot make it. Riverton
has fifteen, and fifteen minutes of human attention is affordable. The principle that scales, though,
is the same at any volume: **the step that leaves the building is the step where a human being
should be, until you have watched the machine do it correctly a hundred times.**

## A short history of the invoice form

The printed invoice is a child of the nineteenth century, and its shape was fixed by the railways and
the telegraph.

Before printing was cheap, an invoice was a handwritten note. When businesses grew, and when the same
customer began buying from several departments of the same supplier, the note became a form: printed
with the company's name, with numbered lines, with columns for quantity, description, price and
amount. The columns are not arbitrary—they mirror the sequence in which a clerk checks the document,
left to right, ending with the total.

The form has survived every technology since, including this one, because it encodes the minimum
information needed to settle a dispute: who, what, how many, at what price, how much tax, in total,
by when. Software did not change the form. It changed the cost of producing it, and—more
importantly—the cost of *finding* it afterwards.

That last change is the one nobody notices. In 1975, a copy of an invoice lived in a filing cabinet,
in a folder, under a customer's name, and finding it took a person who knew the system. In 2026, it
lives in a database and can be found by anybody who can type a customer's name—which means it can be
found by the person who is actually on the telephone, rather than by the person who happens to know
where the folder is.

## The archive

Every document that leaves the building should be findable afterwards, and this is where a small
company's habit and a system's capability usually diverge.

Odoo keeps every posted document and can regenerate its PDF at any time, which means the *file* does
not need to be archived separately: the document is the data, and the PDF is a view of it. That is
elegant, and it is also slightly alarming the first time you hear it—what if the template changes and
the invoice prints differently in a year?

The answer is that it will, and that this is acceptable, because the *content* is what matters and
the content is stored. If your jurisdiction requires the exact PDF as issued, Odoo can attach the
generated PDF to the record when it is posted, and the file is then fixed. Riverton does that for
invoices sent abroad, where the tax authority is fussier, and not for domestic ones.

## The printer, and everything that goes wrong with it

A chapter about printing that did not mention the printer would be a chapter written by somebody who
has never worked in an office.

The printer is the least reliable component of the whole stack, and it is the one that fails at the
moment of maximum urgency—which is the nature of printers. Three things are worth knowing.

**Printing to PDF is not printing.** Every document described in this chapter can be produced as a
PDF without a printer being involved, and for invoices and statements that is what a company should
do: generate the PDF, attach it to an email, and keep the paper for the things that need a
signature. Riverton's printer is used for delivery notes and the Friday stock report, and for
nothing else.

**A document that has been printed is not a document that has been sent.** This sounds obvious and
it is the source of the Ashfield invoice at the beginning of this book. The system knows that the
PDF was generated; it does not know whether the email was sent. If sending matters, the sending has
to be recorded—which is one reason companies eventually automate the whole step, so that the record
exists.

**The paper jam is a business risk.** A delivery note that cannot be printed is a delivery that
cannot leave, because the driver will not go without the paper the customer signs. Every company
that depends on a printer at the loading bay should have a second one, or a way to print the note at
the customer's address. Riverton learned this on a Saturday, and now keeps a spare in a cupboard.

## The three things that go wrong with a print run

Every company that prints documents has met these three, and each has a cheap fix.

**The template changes and nobody notices.** A logo updated, an address changed, a bank detail
corrected—and the template still shows the old one because the change was made on the company record
and the template reads a copy. Odoo's templates read the company record directly, so this is rarer
than in systems that copy; but a custom template can still hard-code a value, and the way to find
out is to print one document after any change and read it.

**The paper is the wrong size.** A delivery note designed for A4 printed on the van's A5 printer
loses its right-hand column—which is where the quantities are. The fix is a second template for the
small format, or a printer that takes A4, and it is worth deciding before the first delivery rather
than after.

**The customer's copy and the file copy diverge.** Somebody writes on the printed copy—a note, a
correction, a signature—and the file keeps the pristine version. That is correct for the invoice and
wrong for the delivery note: the signed note *is* the record, and it should be scanned or filed, not
thrown away because "it is in the system".

## What a signature means

The delivery note comes back with a scribble on it, and the scribble is the most legally interesting
thing in the whole stack.

A signature on a delivery note is an acknowledgement: *I have received these goods, in this
quantity*. It is not a contract—the contract was the order—and it is not a receipt for payment. It
is evidence, and its value is that it was made at the moment of delivery by somebody who was there.

That is why the delivery note is still printed, and why the assistant is not allowed to mark a
delivery as signed. The signature is a physical act by a third party, and no amount of software can
perform it. What software can do is make sure the note that gets signed is the right one: the right
customer, the right order, the right quantities, and a page that the driver can actually read in a
van.

The day the note is signed and comes back, somebody validates the delivery in the system—and the
paper goes into a folder for six years, because that is how long a company may need to prove that
the goods arrived.

## The archive of sent documents

There is a question that a company asks itself the first time a customer disputes an invoice from
two years ago: *can we prove what we sent?*

The answer depends on a decision made long before, and it is worth making deliberately: does the
system keep the *document* or the *data*?

Keeping the data—the invoice record and its lines—means the PDF can be regenerated at any time, and
it will look like today's template rather than the one that was used at the time. For most purposes
that is fine, and it is what Odoo does by default.

Keeping the document means attaching the generated PDF to the record when it is posted, so that the
file is fixed. That costs a few hundred kilobytes per invoice and settles every argument about what
was sent, in the form it was sent.

Riverton attaches the PDF for invoices to customers abroad, where the tax authority is fussier, and
for the two contracts that matter. For domestic invoices it relies on the data, which is a decision
Doreen made after asking her accountant, and which she revisits every year.

---

The binders on the shelf are the exception that proves the rule: the things that must be signed are
still paper, because a signature is still a physical act in most of the world. Everything else has
become a file, and the shelf has not grown in a year.

Doreen noticed that before anybody else did.

"The shelf used to fill up by June," she said. "I'd have to start a new one. This year I've used
about four inches."

She looked at it for a moment.

"It's strange. I spent thirty years making paper, and I don't miss it. What I miss is knowing where
things are—and I've got that back, in a different way."

That is the trade the whole book has been describing, said by the person who made it: not the
disappearance of paper, but the return of knowing where things are, without needing to be the person
who filed them.

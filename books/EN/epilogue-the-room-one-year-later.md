# Epilogue: The Room, One Year Later

This book began in a room full of paper and ends, as these things do, with an invitation.

If you have read this far, you have followed a stationery wholesaler from a Monday morning in
November, when an invoice was driven across town in a car, to a Friday afternoon a year later, when
the same invoice was paid before the weekend. You have seen the installation screens, the
conversations with the assistant, the pages of the ERP, the moments when the system said no. You
have seen the failures too, which is the part that most books leave out and most readers need.

What you have not seen is *your* company. That is the next chapter, and only you can write it.

---

A word about what this book is, and what it is not.

It is not a manual. Manuals are written for people who have already decided, and they assume a
vocabulary. This is an argument, addressed to the person in a small or medium business who suspects
that the way the company works is more expensive than it looks, and who wants to know whether the
new machines—the ones everybody is talking about—can actually help, or whether they are another
round of software that nobody uses.

The answer is that they can help, substantially, and that the reason is not intelligence. It is
that they remove the tax on remembering, and the tax on remembering is the largest single
inefficiency in the ordinary office. A company that stops paying that tax does not become a
technology company. It becomes a company that knows what is true, which is what it always claimed
to be.

It is not a book about Odoo, although Odoo appears on every other page. Odoo is the example, chosen
because it is free, open, serious, and used by millions of businesses in dozens of languages. If
your company already runs a different ERP, most of this book still applies: the model, the
documents, the ledger, the discipline, the limits. The assistant would need a different connector,
and the connector is the smallest part of the work.

And it is not a promise. There is no configuration of software that will make a company run
itself. What there is, and what this book has tried to describe honestly, is a rearrangement: the
machine takes the patience, the person takes the judgement, and the company keeps the record.

---

If you want to try it, the path is shorter than you think, and it is written down.

Appendix A is the installation, step by step, with the screens of the real procedure. It takes an
afternoon on a computer that is not doing anything else, and it asks for one thing you may not
have: a company name. Everything else—the database, the ERP, the assistant, the connector, the
password—the installer arranges for itself.

Appendix B is a glossary, for the words that this book has used and that your colleagues will use
differently. Appendix C is what to do when something goes wrong, which it will. Appendix D is the
library of prompts: every instruction that was executed in the making of this book, grouped by
department, ready to be adapted.

And then there is the QR code below.

It is not decoration. It is the shortest path from this page to the people who build the software
described here. Scan it with a telephone and you will find a channel where questions are answered
by human beings, where the installation problems of other companies are discussed, and where new
versions are announced. If you are stuck at eleven at night with a database that will not start,
that channel is worth more than the rest of this book.

---

One last thing, and then the appendices.

The most common objection to everything in this book is a sentence I have heard a hundred times,
in different words, from different people, in different countries: *my business is different.*

It is. Every business is different, and that is why the software has to be configured, and why the
first month is work. But the objection is almost never about the differences. It is about the
fear—the entirely reasonable fear that changing how the company works will break something that
currently works, and that the person who suggested it will be the one who is blamed.

To that person I would say what Sam Okafor said to Maggie, on a Tuesday morning in a small room
with a photocopier in it: go and look at the warehouse. Count what is actually there. Then ask
yourself how much of what you know about your own company lives in the heads of three people and a
filing cabinet, and what would happen on the day one of those people retired.

The machine will not answer that question for you. But it will make the answer cheap to keep.

And that, in the end, is the whole of it: not a business that runs itself, but a business that
knows itself, and can therefore be run by the people who care about it, with their attention where
it belongs.

The room is still there. The paper is still there. The difference is that somebody, at last, has
written it down.

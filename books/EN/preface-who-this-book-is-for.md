# Preface: Who This Book Is For

This book is for the person who keeps a business running.

Not the person who owns the software company, and not the consultant who sells the system. The
person who arrives at eight in the morning, opens the post, finds the invoice that should have gone
out on Thursday, and spends the next hour finding out why it did not. The office manager, the
bookkeeper, the operations lead, the owner of a firm with eleven employees and a warehouse with
four hundred pallets in it. The person who has heard about artificial intelligence, suspects it
might be relevant, and has no intention of learning to program in order to find out.

If that is you, you need no technical knowledge for what follows. If you can use a web browser,
read an email and answer a telephone, you have everything. There is no code in this book—not a
line, not a command, not a query—and that is a deliberate choice, not a simplification. The people
who need this book are the people who are too busy doing the work to learn a new language.

## What the book promises

Three things.

**It explains what a business system actually is**, in plain language, with the history that makes
the design make sense. By the end you will know what an ERP is, why the ledger has two sides, what
a document is in the software sense, and why any of this matters to a company that sells
stationery.

**It shows the whole installation, screen by screen**, on a real computer, with the real messages,
including the ones that say something has gone wrong. You will see the file you double-click, the
questions it asks, what it does while you wait, and how to check that the result is a working
system rather than a hopeful one.

**It demonstrates an artificial intelligence doing office work in that system**, with the
instruction that was typed and the result that appeared in the ERP, side by side, for every
operation. Quotations, orders, invoices, stock, purchases, reminders, month-end. Not a demonstration
video; a transcript, with screenshots, of work that was actually performed.

## What the book does not promise

It does not promise that your company will run itself. The subtitle is a joke with a serious point:
nothing runs itself. What changes is *who* does which part. The machine takes the patience—the
looking, the retyping, the checking of forty invoices one by one—and the person takes the
judgement. A company that gets that division right becomes calmer, faster and better informed. A
company that expects the machine to take the judgement as well will be disappointed, and will
deserve to be.

It also does not promise that the software is free of faults. The installation in this book hit
real problems, on the real internet, and they are in the chapters that follow rather than hidden in
an appendix. Where a problem was in the software described here, it was fixed and the procedure was
run again; where it was in the network, the book says so. A guide that shows only the successful
path is not a guide.

## How to read it

The chapters run in order, and the argument builds: the problem, the history, the model, the
installation, the work, the prompts, the meaning. If you are impatient—and if you run a business,
you are—read Part II first, install the thing on a spare computer, and come back to the rest with
the screens in front of you. That is how the book was written, and it is a perfectly good way to
read it.

The appendices are reference. Appendix A is the installation as a checklist, without the story.
Appendix B is a glossary. Appendix C is what to do when something goes wrong. Appendix D is the
library of prompts, grouped by department. Appendix E is the legal note about trademarks.

## About the company in these pages

The business is called Riverton Office Supply. It is a demonstration company, created on a real
installation for the writing of this book: a wholesaler with customers, products, a warehouse, a
bank account and a tax office to satisfy. Its people—Maggie, Doreen, Tom, Priya—are invented, and
their dialogue is written, but everything the *software* does in these pages was done, on that
installation, and photographed as it happened.

That distinction matters. The narrative is a story; the screenshots are evidence.

## One more thing

The book is published free, in the hope that it is useful. If it is, the best thing you can do is
send it to somebody who needs it—the person in the office next door who is still driving invoices
across town.

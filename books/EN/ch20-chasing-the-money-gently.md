# 20. Chasing the Money, Gently

There is a document in every accounting system called the *aged debtors report*, and it is the most
unpopular report in the building. It lists every customer who owes money, sorted into columns by how
late they are: not yet due, one to thirty days, thirty-one to sixty, sixty-one to ninety, and the
column nobody wants to be in, over ninety.

Doreen used to produce it monthly and hate it. Not because the numbers were bad—Riverton's customers
paid, mostly—but because producing it took an afternoon, and by the time it existed, it was already
a month out of date. She would telephone the three worst, have three conversations that began
awkwardly and ended with a promise, write the promises on a pad, and then find out two weeks later
that one of the promises had been forgotten by everybody including her.

The first time she asked the assistant for the list, it came back in a second and it was true.

> *Which invoices are overdue, by how many days, and how much is outstanding?*

> *Four invoices are overdue:*
>
> | Customer | Invoice | Due | Days late | Outstanding |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Ashfield Academy | INV/2026/0007 | 14 July | 12 | £1,747.20 |
> | Northgate Library Service | INV/2026/0011 | 2 July | 24 | £612.00 |
> | Calder & Sons | INV/2026/0004 | 20 June | 36 | £2,310.00 |
> | Marchmont Hotel | INV/2026/0002 | 11 June | 45 | £890.40 |
>
> *Total outstanding: £5,559.60. Of which over thirty days: £3,200.40.*

Four invoices. Five and a half thousand pounds. And a list that, for the first time, she could act
on before lunch.

## What an ageing report is for

The report is not a list of bad customers. It is a list of *decisions that have not been made*.

An invoice that is twelve days late is probably an oversight: the invoice arrived while the accounts
department was closing a month, or it went to the wrong inbox, or the purchase order number was
missing and it went into a queue. A telephone call fixes it, and the call should be friendly,
because the customer is not a debtor; they are a customer whose paperwork slipped.

An invoice that is forty-five days late is a different conversation. It may be a dispute, it may be
a company in trouble, and it may be that the invoice was never received. Each of those needs a
different response, and none of them is helped by waiting another month.

An invoice that is over ninety days late is not a collection problem. It is a decision about
whether the customer is worth keeping, and it belongs to the owner of the business, not to the
bookkeeper.

The value of the report is that it sorts the three apart, and the value of a system that produces it
in a second is that it can be produced often enough for the sorting to matter.

## The reminder, and the tone of it

Riverton's first reminder was written by Maggie, in a hurry, and it was too sharp. The second was
written by Doreen, and it was too soft. The third was written by both of them, on a Friday
afternoon, and it is the one the company still uses.

> *Dear [name],*
>
> *Our records show invoice [number] for [amount], dated [date], which was due on [due date]. It may
> of course have crossed with your payment; if so, please ignore this note.*
>
> *If it has not, we would be grateful for payment, or for a word about when to expect it. Our bank
> details are below.*
>
> *Kind regards,*

Three things make it work.

**It assumes the best.** *It may have crossed with your payment* is not a fiction; it is a genuine
possibility, and saying so removes the accusation from the first line.

**It gives the facts.** Number, amount, dates. A reminder that says "your account is overdue" and
nothing else forces the customer to go and look, which is exactly the friction that delays payment.

**It offers a conversation.** *Or for a word about when to expect it* is the sentence that turns a
demand into a dialogue, and it is the sentence that produces payment plans instead of silence.

The assistant writes this letter, filled in from the invoice, in about a second. It writes it in
draft, and Doreen reads it before it goes. That is the arrangement, and it is the right one: the
words are a template, the facts are from the database, and the decision to send is a person's.

## The three-step rhythm

By the autumn, Riverton had a rhythm, and it is the one this book recommends because it is the one
that works without becoming a campaign.

**Day 1 after the due date: nothing.** A day late is not late; it is a bank's clearing time.

**Day 15: a friendly note.** The letter above, by email, with a copy of the invoice attached. No
telephone call yet.

**Day 30: a telephone call.** A person, not a letter, because by day 30 the question is not "did you
receive the invoice" but "is there a problem".

**Day 45: a decision.** Stop supplying until paid, agree a plan, or write it off and tell the
customer why. All three are legitimate; the illegitimate option is to do nothing and hope.

The assistant's job in this rhythm is not to make the calls. It is to produce the list on the right
day, with the right names, and to draft the letters. The judgement—which call to make, what to say,
when to stop supplying—stays with the people who know the customers.

## The customer who pays late by policy

There is a category of late payer that no reminder will fix, and it is worth recognising because it
is more common than the books admit: the customer who pays late *by policy*.

These are usually larger companies, and their accounts department pays on a run—the last Friday of
the month, or the 25th, or whenever the payment file is signed. Your invoice, whatever its terms,
enters a queue, and the queue moves at the speed of the largest company in it. Chasing them is
useless and, if done badly, counterproductive: the person you are telephoning cannot move the queue.

What works with them is different, and it is worth knowing.

**Get on the run.** Ask, once, when their payment run is and what the cut-off is. Then invoice so
that you land before the cut-off. A day's difference in the invoice date can be a month's difference
in the payment date, and it costs nothing.

**Make the paperwork impossible to reject.** Their queue rejects invoices with no purchase order
number, or a missing tax number, or an address that does not match their vendor master. Every
rejection adds a cycle. The assistant helps here in a way that is unglamorous and valuable: it can
check, before the invoice is sent, that the purchase order number is on it and that the address
matches the one on their record.

**Do not chase on day sixteen.** A reminder that arrives while the invoice is in the queue teaches
the accounts department that your reminders are noise, and then the one that matters is ignored too.

Riverton had two customers of this kind, and the change from "chase everything on day fifteen" to
"chase these on day fifteen, those on day thirty-five, and those never" was worth more than any
template. It is a small piece of segmentation, and it is the difference between a process and a
ritual.

## The cost of chasing badly

There is a version of credit control that costs more than the money it recovers, and it is worth
describing because small companies fall into it when they are frightened.

It looks like this: a reminder every three days, by email and telephone, to everybody who is a day
late. A tone that starts formal and becomes cold. A supplier who stops answering the telephone. And
a customer—a good one, with a temporary cash problem—who decides that the relationship is no longer
worth the aggravation and takes their business somewhere with a longer fuse.

The arithmetic is not obvious and it is decisive. A customer worth £40,000 a year of revenue, at a
gross margin of twenty-five per cent, is worth £10,000 of gross profit. Chasing them three days late
on a £900 invoice risks that £10,000 to accelerate £900 by a fortnight. The interest on £900 for two
weeks, at any plausible rate, is about a pound.

That is the calculation the assistant cannot make and a person must. The system can tell you who is
late and by how much; it cannot tell you what the relationship is worth. That judgement lives in the
same place it always did—with the person who knows the customer—and the system's contribution is to
give that person the facts early enough to make the judgement calmly.

## What the assistant is not allowed to do

Riverton's assistant can draft reminders and list overdue invoices. It cannot send them, and the
reason is not technical.

A reminder that leaves the building is a communication with a customer, and a communication has a
tone, a timing and a context that a machine does not know. The system knows the invoice is
thirty-two days late; it does not know that the customer's finance director died in September, which
is the sort of thing that is in Doreen's head and not in the database.

The arrangement—draft, read, send—costs Doreen about four minutes a week, and it is four minutes
well spent. In a company with four hundred overdue invoices, the calculation changes: you automate
the first reminder for the small amounts and keep the human attention for the large ones. That is a
policy decision, and it should be made deliberately, in writing, rather than discovered by a customer
who receives a computer-generated condolence.

## A short history of dunning

The word *dun*—to press a debtor for payment—comes from the Middle English *dunnen*, and it is
related to the sound a creditor makes when he stands in your doorway. It is an old profession: the
Venetians had *sensali* who chased debts across the Mediterranean, and the medieval fair at
Champagne had a procedure for publicly shaming a merchant who did not pay, which was considered
both effective and, within the merchant community, entirely reasonable.

The modern version is regulated: most European countries limit when and how a company may chase a
consumer, and the rules for business-to-business are looser but not absent. What has not changed in
seven hundred years is the fundamental arithmetic of credit: a company that pays late is borrowing
from its supplier, at a price the supplier did not agree to.

For a wholesaler, that arithmetic is the difference between a business and a hobby. Riverton's
average payment time was fifty-three days when the project started, and forty-one after six months
of reminders that went out on time. Twelve days of turnover, in the bank, for the price of a
template and a list.

## The one thing the assistant found that nobody expected

In the third month, Doreen asked a question that had never occurred to her before, because answering
it used to take a day.

> *Which customers owe us money and have also stopped ordering?*

The answer was one name: a garden centre that had been a good customer for four years, owed
£1,240, and had not placed an order since May. Doreen telephoned them expecting a difficult
conversation about money, and found a business in the middle of a change of ownership, with a new
manager who did not know Riverton existed and a filing cabinet full of unopened post.

The invoice was paid that week. The account was saved, and it is still a customer. And the thing
that saved it was not the reminder—it was the *question*, which nobody had been able to ask before
because asking it took a day.

That is the pattern that repeats through this whole book: the system does not make people kinder or
cleverer. It makes the questions cheap enough to ask, and some of the questions turn out to be
worth asking.

---

Doreen's aged debtors report is now produced every Monday, by the assistant, in about four seconds,
and it goes to Priya and Maggie as a list of names with a sentence each.

The list is shorter than it used to be, not because the customers changed but because the *delay*
changed: an invoice that is chased on day fifteen is paid on day twenty, and an invoice that is
chased on day forty-five is paid on day sixty or not at all.

"It's not the letters," Doreen says, when asked. "It's that we send them. We never used to send
them, because it took an afternoon to know who to send them to. So we didn't, and we called it
being nice, and it cost us five thousand pounds a year."

She is right, and the phrase she uses for the old behaviour—*we called it being nice*—is worth
keeping. Most of the money that a small company loses to late payment is lost to a virtue that has
not been thought through.
